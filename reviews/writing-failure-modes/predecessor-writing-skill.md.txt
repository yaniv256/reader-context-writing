# Writing

**After writing any sentence, ask one question: did I write this for the
conversation, or for the reader?**

That is the gate. Nearly every failure catalogued below is a sentence that
reached the page without passing it.

The reason it happens is structural. I write while the discussion is still live —
the objection just answered, the argument just won, the alternative just
rejected, the number just quoted, the decision just made. All of it is vivid to
me and absent for the reader, so it leaks onto the page looking like content.
Nothing about the sentence flags it. It reads fine to the person who was in the
conversation, which is the only person who will never notice.

The gate cannot be applied while drafting, because during drafting the two
contexts feel identical. It has to be applied on a re-read, sentence by
sentence, asking of each: **would this exist if the conversation had not
happened?**

---

## What conversation-context writing looks like

### Defending a decision that nobody has questioned

*X, which is a constraint rather than a convenience.* *This is the honest trade
against Y.* *Worth naming so a reviewer does not think Z.* Each answers a
challenge that was live for me and invisible to the reader — and lands early,
where the decision was made, which is exactly where the reader least understands
what is being defended.

Say what the design is. A reader who wants to challenge it will do so better from
a clear statement than from a pre-emptive rebuttal.

*"You're involving them in an internal debate that you were having with yourself
at the time of the writing."*

### Defending an edit I have just made

The narrowest form: fixing something, then explaining why the fix beats what it
replaced. *The trailing 30 days, rather than a fixed past month.* The reader never
saw the old version.

### Importing the argument that convinced me

A derivation that settled a question in discussion is usually unnecessary in the
document. The reader is being told what the design is, not argued back into it,
and the algebra spends attention the actual content then does not get.

**The tell: a passage that exists because it convinced *me*.** Replace it with the
choice plus one sentence of reason. If something is load-bearing but secondary it
is a side note, never a formula on the main line.

*"This was an argument you made in a conversation and decided it belongs in the
article, where actually I don't think it belongs. We don't need to prove."*

### Writing about what is not being done

Absent data sources, rejected approaches, alternatives nobody proposed — all of
them were live in the conversation and none belong in the document. *"No body
text is used, and no external service is called"* tells the reader nothing they
needed.

The exception is narrow: name a rejected alternative only when it is what a
competent reader would reach for by default — an additive context term, a
semantic embedding of a proper name, a language's built-in string hash. Those are
guardrails. Even then, state the property positively and let the alternative
appear as the reason, not the headline. Genuine limitations are different and
belong in the section whose job is to record them.

*"Just realize that we don't need to talk about what we're not doing."*

### Assuming context the reader does not have

Two failures, both invisible from inside the conversation.

**What the reader knows so far.** A document is read forward. A sentence
depending on material introduced later is not early, it is unreadable where it
appears — a comparison before both things compared exist, a symbol before its
definition, a trade-off between options only one of which has been named.
Headings are the sharpest case, since a heading is read before its content: a
self-referential title like "Why this exists" points at a referent the reader
does not have. Name the subject, not the document. And develop one subject fully
before starting the next; a reader cannot hold an unfinished thread while another
opens.

**What the reader has at all.** A future reader holds neither the deck this
accompanied, the conversation that produced it, nor the ticket that prompted it.
*"The object the slides call…"*, *"as discussed"* — each quietly makes the
document a fragment. Name a companion artifact once if useful; let nothing in the
body depend on having seen it.

*"Both models use it, so neither gains an advantage" — three sections before the
two models are specified. And: "that just assumes the reader knows about the
slides."*

### Deferring instead of writing

*Section 4 recovers exactly that.* *Section 6 takes up what each costs.* *…which
is what section 2 builds.* A pointer stands where a sentence should be, and the
reader is handed a number to hold instead of a fact.

The distinction is whether the sentence is complete without the reference. *The
weights are fitted on the ranking objective (§3.5)* is fine — the parenthetical
is a place to look later. *Section 4 recovers that* is not: its meaning depends
on knowing what section 4 contains, which the reader does not.

**The tell: a forward reference is usually covering for a missing sentence.**
Three of four in one draft were, and writing the actual thing came out shorter
and stronger every time. A section closing on *which is what section 2 builds*
became *those properties are the same for a story published this morning as for
one published last month, so a model built on them keeps working as the pool
turns over* — the real answer to the problem the section had just posed, which
the pointer had been standing in front of.

*"You can't start talking about Section 4 like this. The reader doesn't know
anything about Section 4."*

### Treating the document like a thread

A conversation is append-only; a document is not. When something new has to go
in, the instinct is to find the spot and insert — a paragraph, a note, a formula
between two others. **Rewrite the whole passage instead, so it reads as though
the new material had been there from the start.**

An insertion preserves prose written without knowledge of the new thing, so the
surrounding sentences now describe a document that no longer exists: a definition
the addition contradicts, a transition that no longer connects, a summary that
omits what was just added.

**The tell: if the edit is an insertion, it is probably wrong.** Replacing a
subsection is the normal shape of a revision.

The commonest disguise is the callout — rather than rework a section, add a note
to it. A note is for a hazard a reader must not miss, never for material that was
inconvenient to place. Callouts also decay: at high density they stop signalling
and the real hazards are lost among them. Ask of each, is this a trap someone
would otherwise fall into? If not, it is prose.

*"Every time you wanted to add something you added it as a note. This is not good
writing."*

### Carrying forward the order things were discovered in

An opening that begins with scope, data sources and date ranges is reporting the
sequence of the investigation. The reader has not agreed to read anything yet;
none of that is a reason to decide.

Open with what makes the subject worth someone's time — the observation, the gap,
the question — then say what the document will do about it. **Keep it extremely
short: every word in an opening is paid for in readers who leave.** Roughly a
hundred words, three short paragraphs, before any reference material.

Preliminaries are demoted rather than cut, and often carry motivation once
reframed: *"everything comes from one table we already have"* is not
administrative trivia, it says the work can start now.

*"You don't start an article by talking about the data." And: "every word you
write is paid for with readers that are lost."*

### Freezing a parameter of the investigation into the specification

A window I happened to profile becomes a pinned date range. A cohort I happened
to measure becomes a definition. The specification should say what recomputes and
when; the measured figures are illustration, labelled as such.

## Rules that stand on their own

### Every sentence you keep is a claim you are re-asserting

Rewriting is not moving sentences around. Each sentence carried forward is a
claim made again, in a document that has changed underneath it. **Ask of each: is
this true right now?** "It was there before" is not a reason.

Scrutinise absolutes hardest — *nothing, never, always, only, none*. A claim that
"nothing here is fitted" survived two rewrites that added a fitted constant
directly beneath it.

**A number is not evidence until it bears on the claim.** Check that the
statistic measures the thing being argued about, not a neighbouring population
that happens to be at hand. A real figure deployed for a claim it does not
support is a bluff, and it is worst in an opening where the reader has nothing
else to judge by.

*"You have to think about what you read. Ask yourself, is it true?" And: "you
can't bluff the reader on the second sentence of the article."*

### Claim exactly what is true

- **No inflated commitments.** "one of the things we would add next", not "the
  first thing" — priority ordering is the reader's to set.
- **No invented figures.** A plausible number in a document that otherwise cites
  measurements will be read as measured.
- **No unverified citations.** Author, title, venue, year, each checked against a
  published record before it ships.

### Say the thing; do not announce that you are saying it

No phrases advertising the author's own candour or rigour: "the honest catch",
"to be honest", "let's be clear", "set before results". Label a caveat "Note" and
give the caveat.

*"This is a signature of you as a particular model. If you've got something to
explain, just explain it."*

### Define terms and notation where they first appear

No front-loaded glossary or symbol table. A table read cold is a list of glyphs
and it drifts from the body as the document changes; defining in place also
surfaces collisions a table hides. This covers jargon as much as symbols — terms
like "age" or "reading share" carry a precise local meaning a reader cannot
infer.

*"Your use of the terms age and reading share, that's inside baseball."*

## Reader-context notes

A paragraph is an operator on reader context. It takes the reader in one state
and returns them in another, and it is good exactly insofar as that transition is
the one the document needs at that point. But the input state is invisible — it
lives in my head while drafting and is gone by the time anyone reviews the
result — so the operator gets judged on how it reads rather than on what it does.

The fix is to write the state down. Interleave hidden notes through the document,
each recording two things at that position:

```
Knows:                  what the reader holds coming in — and what they
                        conspicuously do not hold yet
Next passage owes them: the state it must leave them in
```

These are a record of the reasoning that produced the passage, kept beside it.
Not a summary of the passage — a specification of the job.

### Why the pair is the test

The notes surrounding a paragraph form a claim: *given this input, this paragraph
produces that output.* So the note before paragraph N+1 must state the state that
paragraph N's goal line predicted. **Where consecutive notes fail to meet, the
paragraph between them does not do what it claims** — and the mismatch localizes
the defect to one paragraph instead of leaving "this section drags" as the whole
diagnosis.

This makes review answerable. *Is this good writing* has no procedure. *Does this
passage carry the reader from the state above to the state below* does.

### Writing with them

Write the note first, then the passage. Committing to what the reader knows and
what they need next is a decision that would otherwise be made implicitly, and
made worse.

It also exposes the openings. A note reading *they have not decided to read on,
and may read nothing after this* is a hard constraint that a paragraph opening
with scope and data sources visibly violates. Where reader state changes fastest —
openings, the turn from problem to mechanism — one note per paragraph. Where the
passage elaborates something already established, the state does not move enough
to earn one; the unit is **each point where what the reader knows actually
changes**, not each paragraph.

### Using them on review

Read the document through the notes rather than around them:

1. **Does each passage discharge the note above it?** If it delivers something
   else, one of the two is wrong — and it is worth deciding which, because a
   passage doing something better than its stated job means the note was
   mis-specified.
2. **Do consecutive notes meet?** A gap is a missing passage. An overlap is a
   subject developed twice.
3. **Does any note assume something the reader was never given?** This is the
   gate, made checkable: the note names the assumption out loud, where the prose
   only relies on it.

### Keeping them honest

They are scaffolding, not content: hidden from readers, hidden from screen
readers, absent in print. A reader should never encounter one.

**A stale note is worse than no note**, because it asserts the reader's state
falsely and review then proceeds from a false premise. Rewriting a passage means
rewriting its note in the same edit. They cannot be added at the end either — a
note reconstructed from finished prose describes what the paragraph does, which
is the one thing the method must not assume.

*"This is like a record of the chain of thought that was used to create that
paragraph. The paragraph is like an operator that we apply on reader context. We
need to expose what is our understanding of reader context at every point in
order to evaluate whether the operator works or not."*

## The practice

Apply the gate on a re-read, not while drafting. Then read the whole document top
to bottom, repeatedly. Local edits each look fine; structural damage only appears
on a full pass — a section whose order broke because one part grew, a term used
two sections before it is explained, two sections that now disagree. There is no
shortcut.

Before declaring it done:

- every sentence would exist even if the conversation had not happened
- the opening earns attention in under a hundred words
- every claim is true of the document as it now stands, absolutes most of all
- every number bears on the claim it is attached to
- every term and symbol is defined once, at or before first use
- no section contradicts another, and no heading refers to the document
- each subject is developed once, not revisited
- every callout is a hazard; the rest is prose
- nothing describes what is not being done
- nothing from the investigation is frozen into the specification
- no forward reference stands where a sentence belongs
- every passage discharges the note above it, and consecutive notes meet
- no note is stale, and no reader can see one
